Question 03 — MSc dissertation

Why do solar panels hate the sun?

Every degree above 25 °C costs a silicon cell up to half a percent of its output — the sunniest hour is also the least efficient. My dissertation designs a way out: a photovoltaic-thermal (PVT) panel that pumps water behind the cells, harvesting the waste heat and keeping the silicon cool enough to do its job.

RoleGraduate researcher
ToolsSOLIDWORKS · ANSYS CFD · Polysun · Excel
ContextMSc Renewable Energy Engineering, Kingston University · supervised by Dr Hossein Mirzaii
Year2023–24

The problem, made visible.

The panel was modelled layer by layer — ETFE front sheet, EVA encapsulant, monocrystalline cells, PET, adhesive, carbon-fibre backing — and simulated under solar load in ANSYS. Uncooled, the stack settles above 53 °C: an efficiency loss of 11–14% exactly when irradiance peaks.

Same geometry, same sun. The only difference is the fluid loop.

Designed part by part.

The panel and its integrated heat exchanger were built as native SOLIDWORKS parts and assembled into a full PVT module — then meshed and verified for simulation quality (skewness 0.617) before any results were trusted.

Then tested against a real postcode.

The module was dropped into a whole-building Polysun model for Wandsworth, London — real irradiance, real hot-water demand — and compared across three system configurations. The harvested heat preheats domestic hot water: 835 kWh of useful thermal energy a year on top of the electrical yield.

Honest about its limits.

Everything here lives in simulation — idealised weather, no manufacturing tolerances, no leaks. The dissertation says so, and maps the next step: a physical prototype, dynamic weather models, and advanced coolants (nanofluids, phase-change materials) to hold efficiency through winter. That prototype is the work I want to do next.
